Brief Overview

  • Prerequisite
    • It is assumed that you have gone through the first ramp.
    • If not, you should atleast know that neural networks (NNs) are universal function approximators and how they are trained.
  • Main Problem
    • Humans have imagination power. We can imagine literally anything, be it some known or novel concept or physical object. Let’s denote whatever concept or physical object we can imagine as \(x\) for simplicity.
    • Novel \(x\) is some \(x\) which is atleast according to you does not exist yet in the world but also it may not really exist for anyone. We create useful stuff with the help of our imagination power.
    • We can do two kind of imaginations - “wild” and “problem-driven”. We use problem-driven imagination when we want to solve a problem. Given a problem, we only think and imagine those \(x\) which can atleast solve a problem to some extent instead of not solving it at all. Let’s call such viable but not necessarily perfect \(x\) “candidate”. So, problem-driven imagination can be defined as an imagination where space of imagination is constrained to only candidates of a given problem. We do not think of those \(x\) which are not candidate.
    • E.g. a problem is that you want to buy a shirt and you are not sure what kind of shirt you are looking for. But ofcourse you have some preferences. Like you do not want to repeat the shirts you have in your wardrobe. And atleast you also have size or fitting preferences. Let’s say you have just these preferences. What you will do? You will go to online or offline stores. But before going there you will think and imagine many shirts on you one after another which follow these preferences. Here any shirt which follow the preferences is a candidate but a car is not a candidate or any other shirt which does not follow the preferences is not a candidate. This explains what we mean by “candidate”. So, the space of imagination contain only shirts and all kinds of shirts which follow preferences. By imagining, you are trying to find what next shirt you should buy.
    • On the other hand, one does “wild” imagination when someone does not want to solve a problem but still imagines. Then in this case there is no constrain in the space of imagination. It is borderless. One can imagine literally any \(x\).
    • Wild imagination is not dependent on one particular problem. It is unhinged. The space of imagination has no boundaries. So we are free to imagine anything. And since space of imagination is just infinite, there are high chances that whatever we are imagining may not lead to solving one of many real problems out there. Example of such imagination is dreaming, be it night or day dreaming.
    • Relative to wild imagination, problem-driven imagination is very clearly useful because there is a problem which we are focused on solving. And there is space of imagination which is constrained or fixed. Since, we are focused on solving and space is fixed, we can easily imagine a candidate which will atleast help us solve a problem instead of not helping at all. For easier problems, problem-driven imagination can actually solve the problem. E.g. designers of all sorts be it physical product designers, architects, web designers etc. use imagination power to solve a problem.
    • On the other hand, scientists deals with problems which are extremely difficult. For such problems, problem driven imagination is very difficult to do it effectively and hence one do not form a complete solution via imagination. But atleast imagination help them to propose a theory and hypothesis which they can test whether it is solving a problem or not. Einstein once imagined riding on a beam of light to better understand light.
    • But [As far as I know. May edit this para later if required] there are many problems like drug discovery, material discovery, biological discovery etc. where problem-driven imagination is only barely more useful than wild imagination. Because such problems have exponentially large space of imagination. Take an example of drug discovery problem. Given some disease, there are exponential number of candidates. And one cannot sweep all the candidates and think of a novel molecule which is better than existing molecules. It is impossible for a human to do that because of limited time or capacity. This is just an example. Many real-world problems like mentioned above which requires imagination are very difficult to solve because space of imagination is exponential.
    • As mentioned, wild imagination is not clearly useful because of infinite space of imagination. But atleast problem-driven imagination has not infinite space. So let’s first focus on problem-driven imagination and let’s see if we can do problem-driven imagination when it is hard to do. Then later on, we can talk about improving wild imagination too in some other g-ramp.
    • What if we can let computer do problem-driven imagination and create novel things which are useful. Why computer? Because of its speed, accuracy, and scalability (in term of space of imagination).
    • When we let computer imagine, the algorithm which does it is called “Generative AI” algorithm or “gen AI” algorithm in short. And “generate” term is used when a computer imagine something.

    • We can let gen AI algorithm generate \(x\) like molecules etc. in terms of data structures like images, graph, tree etc.

    • Now, we have come to our “main problem” of this g-ramp. “Main problem” which this g-ramp is for, is that we want a computer to do “problem-driven imagination”.
    • We can make the “main problem” more concrete so that it is easy for us to think of solution. But our solution should be for the “main problem” and not just for its concrete version.
    • To make it concrete, we can think of some particular problem for which we want a computer to do problem-driven imagination. For simplicity, let’s choose a problem where we want a computer to imagine or generate digits in different handwriting.
  • LS - Two Functions of Intelligence
    • Since we want to let computer do imagination, first obvious step should be that we should first understand how humans learn to do imagination?
    • [Explain that we mentioned in previous ramp that intelligence meaning having two functions - learning and action. And imagination is one kind of action function of human intelligence. And there should be a learning function which outputs an action function for imagination]
    • We also mentioned that, to simulate these two functions in a computer we need to implement them via mathematics.
    • Let’s first try to understand these two functions with respect to the human action - imagination or more specifically problem-driven imagination because that is what our “main problem” demands. That is, how a human learn to do problem-driven imagination and how they do this imagination, once they have learned to do it.
    • And then we can think of formulating them in mathematics.
  • LS - How a human learn to imagine?
    • [Explain that we do not learn it consciously. It is inherent and already there naturally.]
    • But one thing, we can say that imagination becomes mature or more controlled with time, as we gain more knowledge of the world.
    • Why? Because we understand more and more regularity in the world. We understand that there are representations or description of the world at different level. We will be using the term “representation” to mean description, just like in previous ramp.
      • [mention that we have explained representation in previous ramp]
    • And as we see the world around, representation at every level changes at different rate. As we move from highest level to lowest level, the rate of change of representation increases from 0 to highest.
    • As toddlers grow, they understand more and more things. With time, they understand basic physics, shape, texture, color, material, position, posture, pose, light etc.
    • [Explain that we can represent or describe at any level literally anything in the world. We represent whatever we focus on. We can describe a scene containing multiple objects or just single object]
      • [give examples explaining this]
      • Scene here is nothing but whatever we focus on.

    • [explain that more higher-level the representation is of, more number of different scenes can be described by single representation]

    • E.g. we are focusing on a shirt. So, scene is nothing but that shirt. We describe that shirt. Lowest level of representation describes it in most detail which includes everything what we are seeing including the exact posture, background description etc.
    • A little higher representation will exclude the posture and background description. Such representation just describes that shirt. It is independent of its posture and background. Such representation let’s imagine that shirt in different scenes which we haven’t seen before, i.e., in different postures and background.

    • As we see more and more shirts, we notice that they come in different color, material, pattern, collar, fitting, sleeves etc. And we notice that all these descriptions of a shirt does not define shirt. And we figure out a description or representation which describes all the shirts in any posture, background, color, material, pattern etc. This is the highest level of representation if we only want to describe a single object, here shirt, of the world.

    • [talk about how this explains problem-driven imagination?]
      • [Give the example of shirt problem. We have worn and seen a lot of shirts.]

    • Another example for understanding different levels of representation is imagining a particular cup on a table. After you observe this cup at different positions and in different orientation on a table, you will know that highest level of representation only describing cup and table is that “there is this cup on a table”. And lower level representations describes cup’s different orientations and different position on the table.

    • [now, explain different levels of representation for a drug design problem and how understanding this will help us generating all possible drugs]

    • [conclude that learning to imagine means understanding different levels of representations]
    • [conclude that for problem-driven imagination, one learns a high-level representation, let’s call it “problem-level representation”, which describes all the candidates. Also explain that this representation is just high-enough that it only describes all candidates and nothing else apart from this.]
      • Different problems will have different problem-level representation.
  • LS - How a human imagine?
    • When we try to solve a problem via problem-driven imagination, we do not just imagine one candidate. But we imagine multiple candidates that we think could solve the problem because we do not really know what is the best solution. We may think, there could be even better candidate which we have not imagined yet and this is the reason we keep imagining.
    • [given a problem, explain that to imagine a candidate, one need to create lower levels of representation until the lowest level representation by “adding” some more description on top of problem-level representation]
      • Let’s call lowest level representation as “candidate-level representation”.
      • [Explain that candidate-level representation describes a particular candidate]
    • [and explain that the description is chosen first randomly and then added to problem-level representation]
      • [give examples]
      • In shirt example, we can choose color, pattern, fitting randomly out of many options.

    • Given a problem, action function of imagination just brings to your mind the candidate \(x\) one by one. And there is no fixed order. Whenever you try to solve same problem by problem-driven imagination you will imagine its candidates in different order every time. As if, a candidate to imagine is chosen randomly. And when you keep imagining, you are keep picking next candidate randomly from space of imagination.

    • After imagining many candidates, you know which candidates are potentially better than other candidates. But only after rigourously testing these potential candidates, we select the candidate or candidates which solve the problem in a better manner. Testing the candidates would involve manifesting them in some form so that one can consciously think about them.

    • [give examples]
    • Let’s continue our shirt example from “Main Problem” section. We would like to imagine many shirts. We know space of imagination is large. And we know there is possibility that we can imagine a shirt which is better than the already imagined shirts. When we try to imagine a shirt, it seems like we just randomly choose a candidate to imagine out of all candidates. Same process happens whenever we try to change our current imagination and try to imagine another shirt.
      • After imaginging many shirts one after another, you have in mind some shirts which are potentally better than other shirts. So now you would like to test these shirts properly by finding these shirts or similar shirts in online or offline stores. And try them on. And then only you can finally decide whether to buy which shirts out of these potential shirts.

    • Another example is web designing. You imagined some web designs. But to decide finally whether which design is good, you create a mockup and test it.

    • [now explain that similar process happens in drug design. Drug testing would involve manifesting them first, which is very expensive but also accurate.   • LS - Mathematical Formulation of Functions
    • We have understood two functions - action and learning with respect to human action - problem-driven imagination.
    • Now we should try to formulate them in mathematics.
    • [As explained in previous ramp, to mathematically formulate the two functions of intelligence, we have to first formulate action function and then we can formulate learning function accordingly.]
    • When we think of action function described in previous step, it immediately reminds us a problem of picking a card from shuffled deck. One can pick any card out of \(52\). Each card have \(1/52\) probability of getting picked.

    • Similarly, one can imagine any candidate from space of imagination. It means, each next candidate have \(1/\vert S \vert\) probability of getting picked for imagination, where \(\vert S \vert\) is size of space of imagination.

    • Let’s represent probability over \(x\) as \(p(x)\). In problem-driven imagination, \(x\) is a candidate. While in wild imagination, \(x\) is just \(x\), i.e., there is no constrain.
    • [define “random variable”]
    • So mathematically, we can say, action function is nothing but sampling from this probability distribution.
      • [define what we mean by “probability distribution” and “sampling” for a refresher. This is covered in school-level maths.]

    • For a given problem, a computer does not know the probability distribution over candidates because it does not know all the candidates. Let’s call this unknown target probability distribution as \(T\).

    • When we try to approximate \(T\) in a computer or in mathematical form, that mathematical form is called “model” (similarly explained in first ramp).
      • [And may be we can explain more about what do we mean by word “model”]
      • We can denote model by \(M\).
    • If we have a model of \(T\), then we can let a computer sample from \(M\) because we know this distirbution unlike \(T\). And this is how we can implement action function.
      • [explain how to sample from given probability distribution?]
    • [also mention and explain the term “data generating process”]

      • Numbering the pixels starting from start of the wave. And iterating in the order defined above.

    • It means, we are adding some error and hence making the distribution to not “overfit” the data.
      • [we will be explaining “overfit” term in first ramp]
    • Or we can say we are making the modes of conditional distribution less spiky by adding some error.
